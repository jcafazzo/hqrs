# Reading-to-lecture map

All four required and nine optional readings were reviewed against the introductory lecture's intended learner transformation.

## Required readings

### 1. Cafazzo & St-Cyr — *From Discovery to Design* (2012)

**Core contribution:** the historical and moral spine of the lecture. It connects no-blame/just culture, use error, the Hierarchy of Intervention Effectiveness, and the move from identifying problems after the fact to designing safer systems upstream.

**Deck alignment:** very high. The lecture already contains its main ideas and figures.

**2026 role:** use its argument more explicitly. The best update is not another example but a clearer through-line from fallibility to system-focused design.

### 2. Swensen et al. — *Cottage Industry to Postindustrial Care* (2010)

**Core contribution:** healthcare's variability, weak standardization, fragmented delivery, and the need for coordinated systems of care.

**Deck alignment:** supports frames 70–96 and the healthcare-versus-other-industries comparison.

**2026 role:** retain the system argument but soften the binary comparison. Healthcare differs from aviation and nuclear power in work variability, feedback, standardization, and regulatory reach; it is not simply “unregulated.”

### 3. Cafazzo — *Design for Digital Health* (2020)

**Core contribution:** the design gap, human-tech ladder, four orders of design, ethnography, human-factors-informed design, and the transition from products to services.

**Deck alignment:** extremely high; much of the second half closely follows this chapter.

**2026 role:** this is the source of the deck's richness and its current scope problem. Select the elements that support the introductory thesis; move the deeper product/service-design material to the service-design session or appendix.

### 4. Holden et al. — *SEIPS 2.0* (2013)

**Core contribution:** a rigorous work-system model linking person(s), tasks, tools/technology, organization, internal and external environments, work processes, outcomes, and adaptation.

**Deck alignment:** conceptually essential but visually underused.

**2026 role:** make SEIPS—or its simplified Human-Tech Ladder expression—the recurring visual structure. Each major case should visibly add system conditions around the person.

## Optional readings

### 1. Gawande — *Why Doctors Hate Their Computers* (2018)

**Core contribution:** digitization as sociotechnical reorganization, clerical burden, loss of patient attention, and workarounds.

**Deck alignment:** supports the EHR/fax and scribe sequence.

**2026 role:** retain as a strong narrative reading, but update the slide example from interface ugliness to a closed-loop failure in a contemporary EHR or e-prescribing system.

### 2. Khullar — *Bad Hospital Design Is Making Us Sicker* (2017)

**Core contribution:** the physical environment affects infection, stress, sleep, staff work, and recovery.

**Deck alignment:** the environment is named but does not receive a modern clinical example.

**2026 role:** pair with temporary/corridor care or a paediatric mental-health ward. This makes “environment” a system component rather than an architectural aside.

### 3. Chapanis — *Psychology and the Instrument Panel* (1953)

**Core contribution:** the classic origin story: apparently careless operators repeatedly make predictable errors when controls are poorly differentiated.

**Deck alignment:** foundational to the deck's object theatre and aviation lineage.

**2026 role:** keep because it is canonical. Label it as the historical birth of the discipline, then immediately show a contemporary analogue.

### 4. Croskerry — *Achieving Quality in Clinical Decision Making* (2002)

**Core contribution:** cognitive biases, heuristics, workload, fatigue, and strategies for diagnostic reasoning.

**Deck alignment:** supports cognition, expertise, and fatigue, but the deck currently gives these less structure than design examples.

**2026 role:** use one bias carefully within a work system. Avoid making cognition an individual defect; show how time pressure, information display, and team structure amplify or dampen it.

### 5. Morita & Cafazzo — *Challenges and Paradoxes of Human Factors in Health Technology Design* (2016)

**Core contribution:** paradox of expertise, preference versus performance, and paradox of choice.

**Deck alignment:** directly represented in frames 207–224.

**2026 role:** keep one or two paradoxes, not all three, unless this becomes its own segment. Preference versus performance is the strongest bridge to usability testing.

### 6. Melnick et al. — EHR usability, task load, and burnout (2020)

**Core contribution:** physician-perceived EHR usability is associated with task load and burnout; task load mediates part of the relationship.

**Deck alignment:** supports the EHR, scribe, and fatigue material with stronger evidence than headline screenshots.

**2026 role:** replace old burnout infographics with one clean causal diagram and one honest effect statement. Do not imply that EHR usability alone explains burnout.

### 7. Taylor-Adams, Vincent & Stanhope — applying human-factors methods to clinical adverse events (1999)

**Core contribution:** systematic investigation of active and latent factors, organizational context, and movement away from person-focused blame.

**Deck alignment:** tightly aligned with the case-study method and the course's incident-analysis assignments.

**2026 role:** use as the bridge from the introductory lecture into the three adverse-event workshops. It makes the lecture method consequential for the rest of the course.

### 8. Lan et al. — using the Hierarchy of Intervention Effectiveness (2025)

**Core contribution:** a current UHN quality-improvement project integrating the HIE into critical-incident review. System-focused recommendations increased from a 16% baseline average to a 30% post-intervention average.

**Deck alignment:** ideal contemporary anchor for frames 184–189 and the course's assessment method.

**2026 role:** make this the proof that naming an intervention hierarchy changes the quality of recommendations. State the limits: no sustained special-cause attribution and no demonstrated causal reduction in preventable harm.

### 9. Toomaari & Cafazzo — *Contrasts in Safety Management* (2019)

**Core contribution:** compares healthcare with other safety-critical industries across regulation, operations, design/manufacturing, and safety-management systems.

**Deck alignment:** the source for much of frames 70–96.

**2026 role:** retain the framework but reduce the moral hierarchy. Use it to ask what safety-management capabilities are present, absent, or difficult to transfer into heterogeneous clinical work.

## Overall implication

The reading set is coherent. The deck's problem is not missing theory; it is that almost every good idea from the readings has been admitted onto the stage. The 2026 introduction should make SEIPS/Human-Tech Ladder the **diagnostic lens**, the Hierarchy of Intervention Effectiveness the **action lens**, and `USE ERROR` the **language students carry forward**. The other readings then deepen later sessions instead of competing in the opening lecture.
