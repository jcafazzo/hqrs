# HQRS 846 introductory lecture — adversarial review

## Verdict

**Keep the operating system; edit the content inventory.**

The deck's Jobs/TED DNA is genuine and remains effective: hard visual cuts, object stories, short aphorisms, suspense, humour, and presenter-dependent meaning. The problem is accretion. The 361 exported frames now contain three strong but competing lectures: human error and safety, human-centred product design, and a service-design/Healthcare Human Factors portfolio. A cosmetic reskin would make that diffusion more polished, not less visible.

The 2026 refresh should make one argument unmistakable:

> People will err. Systems shape whether error is likely, visible, recoverable, or harmful.

## What is already excellent

- **Frames 2–8:** the Miss Universe cold open invites an attribution to the person, reveals the poorly designed card, and then lands on “Humans are fallible. We will err.” It makes the room revise its first judgment.
- **Frames 17–25:** patient voices turn the insulin pump into an emotional object rather than a technical abstraction.
- **Frames 41–42:** the fax/EHR contrast lands as a proper punchline.
- **Frames 46–50:** the AED sequence shows observable behaviour instead of merely claiming poor usability.
- **Frames 127–146:** the pill-bottle and EpiPen sequences are excellent object theatre.
- **Frames 157–177:** authentic UHN fieldwork gives backstage access to human-factors practice.
- **Frames 225–242:** the radiation interface sequence closes the loop from design problem to changed performance.
- **Frames 278–280:** the product/service distinction is spatially memorable.
- **Frames 353–354:** the return to the opening aphorism gives the lecture a circular structure.

These are not remnants to tolerate. They are the lecture's signature assets.

## Stagecraft and pacing

The nominal cadence is 361 click states in 120 minutes—about one frame every 20 seconds before videos and discussion. Build states are not inherently excessive. The issue is that too many builds introduce another example instead of deepening the current one.

Use four recurring beat types:

1. **Claim:** one sentence.
2. **Case:** one person, object, or scene.
3. **Evidence:** one number or one cropped detail.
4. **Reflection:** one question with a visible pause.

Insert a participatory reset every 12–18 minutes. With 44–45 learners, nine groups of approximately five can make a quick causal choice, add one system factor, or propose one low- and one high-leverage intervention.

## Visual-system audit

The deck's sampled cyan is approximately `#08BFD5`. White text on that cyan is only about **2.23:1 contrast**. Retain the colour as a signature, but pair it with near-black text. When white text is required, use a dark teal such as `#006C75`.

Recommended palette:

- Ink `#0B0D0F`
- Warm white `#F7F7F5`
- HHF cyan `#08BFD5` with ink text
- Dark teal `#006C75` with white text
- Coral `#FF5A52` only for hazard, blame, or a violated expectation

For 1920×1080 delivery, use approximately 88–116 px for hero statements, 64–80 px for headlines, 38–46 px for supporting text, and 24–28 px for citations. Centred type should be reserved for axioms and punchlines; most explanatory material should be left-aligned.

Motion should remain sparse: hard cuts for claims; a 350–450 ms critically damped move for spatial explanation; a 180–250 ms dissolve for emotional photo changes; and one causal layer per click. No bounce, decorative movement, paragraph animation, or uninterruptible sequences.

## Three redesign directions

### 1. The Forensic Thread — recommended

One current clinical event recurs throughout the lecture. The room first sees only an apparently obvious action, then progressively maps the physical, psychological, team, organizational, and policy conditions. The same case is used to test mitigation strength and closes with a redesigned system.

This best guarantees the intended learning outcome and provides the strongest narrative coherence.

### 2. The Museum of Designed Error

Build the lecture around six physical objects: insulin pump, EHR/fax, AED, pill bottle, EpiPen, and PPE. Each receives the same five beats: what the person saw, what they tried, what the system assumed, why the action was predictable, and what stronger design changes.

This is the most recognizably Jobs-like direction and the cleanest visual refresh.

### 3. Systems Theatre

Turn the room into a live investigation. Freeze three cases before the outcome, let nine groups choose an apparent cause, then reveal system layers and ask for low- versus high-leverage responses. The slide records how the room's explanation changes.

This is the most interactive direction and uses the graduate cohort's experience as course material.

## A tighter two-hour architecture

- **0–12 min — The attribution trap:** preserve the Miss Universe cold open; introduce `USE ERROR`, not `USER ERROR`.
- **12–30 min — Human performance is conditional:** fatigue, workload, cognition, and the environment; one group reflection.
- **30–52 min — See the system:** introduce SEIPS/Human-Tech Ladder through one contemporary case.
- **52–67 min — Object theatre:** retain two compact design cases, ideally AED plus pill bottle/EpiPen.
- **67–72 min — Reset/break.**
- **72–92 min — Choose interventions:** use the Hierarchy of Intervention Effectiveness and the 2025 Lan et al. paper.
- **92–108 min — Evidence that redesign changes performance:** retain the strongest radiation-interface before/after sequence.
- **108–117 min — Resilience, not only failure:** use a near miss where frontline staff caught a system failure.
- **117–120 min — Bookend:** “People will err. Systems decide what happens next.” Return to `USE ERROR`.

## Evidence guardrails

- Avoid saying that systems eliminate individual responsibility. The distinction is between blame and learning; separate safety analysis from processes that determine culpability.
- Prefer: **“Systems shape the conditions under which action occurs.”** Avoid an absolute claim that every error has one system cause.
- Do not treat a model's predictive accuracy as proof of clinical benefit.
- Do not convert incident reports into incidence estimates.
- When showing a redesign, distinguish improved task performance from demonstrated reduction in patient harm.
- The 2025 Lan et al. project increased system-focused recommendations from a 16% baseline average to a 30% post-intervention average, but did not show sustained special-cause variation or a causal reduction in preventable harm.

## What must remain sacred

- The non-blaming moral stance.
- “Humans are fallible. We will err.”
- The cold open that lets the audience make, then revise, a judgment.
- Presenter-led minimalism rather than handout-style slides.
- Real clinical artifacts, behaviour, and UHN fieldwork.
- Humour and provocation without humiliating the person who erred.
- Hard cuts, silence, pause, and reveal.
- Authentic patient and clinician voices.
- Before/after evidence that design changes performance.
- Teal as a recognizable signature, with corrected contrast.
- The final movement from blame to possibility.
