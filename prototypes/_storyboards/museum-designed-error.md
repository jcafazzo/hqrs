# Prototype B — The Museum of Designed Error

## The promise

**A genuinely independent 29-minute opening for HQRS 846**
**Audience:** 44–45 graduate clinicians and life-science professionals
**Learner transformation:** from “Who failed?” to “What made this action seem reasonable, and what should the system do next?”

This direction turns three clinical objects into three exhibits. Each object is first presented as self-evident. A reveal then makes the apparently irrational action understandable. The recurring ritual is:

1. **Look** — what did the person actually see?
2. **Act** — what did they reasonably do?
3. **Widen** — what designed conditions shaped that action?
4. **Change** — what could prevent, detect, or help recovery?

The order matters. Doloral teaches **designed meaning**. Pulse oximetry teaches **designed measurement**. The automated dispensing cabinet teaches **designed recovery**. The emotional movement is blame → unease → systems sight → possibility.

## Stage language

- Use Queen’s Blue (`#002452`) as the museum wall and White as the evidence plane. Gold (`#FABD0F`) marks a signal or recovery; Red (`#B90E31`) marks a violated expectation, never a person.
- One object occupies 55–75% of the 16:9 field. Preserve believable physical scale: a medicine label should feel hand-sized, the pulse oximeter finger-sized, and the ADC drawer body-sized. Avoid browser screenshots.
- Build the three exhibit objects from scratch. The Doloral display must be labelled **re-creation**. For the ADC, use a drawer-front reconstruction plus the two discordant medication forms; do not imply access to the unidentified hospital's device.
- Put sources in a restrained 24–28 px line at bottom left. The evidence belongs in presenter notes and the companion page, not in dense projected prose.
- Use hard cuts to enter an exhibit; 200 ms dissolves only when the object remains fixed and the interpretation changes. Use one 400 ms lateral widening move for each “system” reveal. No decorative motion.
- If physical props are available, place a pulse oximeter and a reconstructed medication label on a black plinth downstage. Pick up the object only after its first projected close-up. The object is the co-presenter.
- Silence is part of the design: hold the first image of each exhibit for three seconds before speaking.

## 22 narrative beats + 6 observation states — 29:00 total

Each numbered entry remains a narrative beat. Six full-screen `OBSERVE` states now follow the Doloral display, authorized fields, both FDA pages, ADC frameshift, and staged-deployment figure. Where an element separates, fades, or shifts **within** an entry, it is a short automatic entrance triggered by that advance.

### Exhibit entrance — establish the ritual (0:00–0:55)

#### 1. Doors open — 0:00–0:25

**On slide:** Almost black. A small museum label in the lower left:

> HQRS 846 · GALLERY 01
> **THE MUSEUM OF DESIGNED ERROR**

**Composition:** An empty illuminated plinth occupies the lower third. No title animation.
**Speaker beat:** Walk to the plinth, look at it, then at the room. “I brought you three objects. Each has been accused of witnessing a human error.” Pause. “Let’s look before we judge.”
**Purpose:** The promise is theatrical, not explanatory. Do not state the systems thesis yet.

#### 2. The exhibit rule — 0:25–0:55

**On slide:** All four words are present on entry; a brief automatic light sweep establishes the reading order:

> LOOK → ACT → WIDEN → CHANGE

**Speaker beat:** “For each exhibit: tell me what you see. Tell me what action makes sense. Then we widen the frame.”
**Visual:** Each word occupies one quadrant; only the active word is warm white, the rest charcoal.
**Caution:** Do not call this a model. It is a performance ritual that prepares students for SEIPS later.

### Exhibit 01 — Designed meaning: Doloral (0:55–5:40)

#### 3. The label — 0:55–1:35

**On slide:** A precisely reconstructed drug-information display, isolated at approximately 1:1 visual scale:

> **DOLORAL 1** &nbsp;&nbsp; 1 mg/mL

Small footer: `Re-creation from an incident report · ISMP Canada`
**Speaker beat:** Say nothing for three seconds. “Read the concentration to the person beside you. Just the concentration.”
**Audience micro-beat:** Let the murmur happen; do not solicit confessions or identify who read what.
**Evidence:** ISMP Canada reported that proximity of the numeral in the product name and the concentration contributed to misinterpretation.

#### 4. What the display appeared to say — 1:35–2:20

**On slide:** The gap between the two numerals collapses. The rest falls away:

> **11 mg/mL**

Below, after a 700 ms automatic delay:

> An 11-fold dose.

**Speaker beat:** “Several reports described this reading. One reported incident resulted in severe patient harm.” Hold the silence; do not dramatize the patient.
**Evidence guardrail:** Say **several reports**, not a rate or incidence. The bulletin does not establish how often the product was used or when each event occurred.

#### 5. Widen the frame — 2:20–3:25

**On slide:** Keep the display fixed in the centre. Widen laterally to reveal five connected layers:

> BRAND NAME · CONCENTRATION · DATABASE · DISPLAY · CLINICAL ACTION

**Speaker beat:** “The action happened at a screen. But the screen inherited a name authorized in a database, placed it beside a concentration, and made two separate ones look like eleven.”
**Question, rhetorical:** “Where, exactly, is the error?”
**Caution:** Do not imply that one display alone caused every report. The source describes the proximity as **a key contributing factor**.

#### 6. The system changed — 3:25–4:20

**On slide:** Before/after, but never as a dashboard:

> ~~DOLORAL 1~~ → **DOLORAL**

Small line:

> The numeral was removed from the product name.

**Speaker beat:** “The response was not: remind every future clinician to look harder. The manufacturer removed the numeral from both product names, in communication with Health Canada.”
**Meaning:** Show a stronger design response without claiming it eliminated all medication risk.
**Source:** ISMP Canada, 20 February 2025.

#### 7. Name the lens — 4:20–5:40

**On slide:** Start with `USER ERROR`. The coral **R** separates and moves off the edge. Behind `USE`, a faint system field appears:

> ~~USE**R** ERROR~~
> **USE ERROR**

Then one small question:

> What made this reading likely?

**Speaker beat:** “Removing the R does not remove the person from the system. It removes the person as the end of the explanation.” Pause. “For medical devices, the FDA calls it a *use error* when the outcome of use differs from what was intended, is not due to device malfunction, and resulted—or could have resulted—in harm. A poorly designed device or a situation that promoted incorrect use may contribute.”
**Caution:** Do not say accountability is irrelevant. Safety learning and adjudication of culpability are different processes.

### Exhibit 02 — Designed measurement: pulse oximetry (5:40–13:25)

#### 8. The reassuring number — 5:40–6:20

**On slide:** A real-size pulse oximeter on a finger. The only bright element is:

> **94%**

No skin-tone comparison yet.
**Speaker beat:** Three seconds of silence. “What does this number permit you to stop worrying about?” Take two spontaneous answers.
**Object theatre:** If using a physical pulse oximeter, place it on your finger now—do not turn the sequence into a live accuracy demonstration.

#### 9. The number and the blood — 6:20–7:25

**On slide:** The object stays fixed. A second number appears below it:

> Pulse oximeter: **92–96%**
> Arterial blood: **<88%**

Label: `Occult hypoxemia · study definition`
**Speaker beat:** “In a 2020 retrospective study, the screen could look reassuring while an arterial sample showed hypoxemia.”
**Caution:** Explain that SpO₂ is an estimate and SaO₂ from arterial blood was the study comparator. Do not imply that every 94% reading is unsafe or that pulse oximetry is useless.

#### 10. Unequal invisibility — 7:25–8:35

**On slide:** Two large figures, no people-as-icons:

> **11.7%** &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **3.6%**
> Black patients &nbsp;&nbsp; White patients

Small line:

> University of Michigan paired measurements with SpO₂ 92–96%

**Speaker beat:** “Occult hypoxemia appeared in 88 of 749 paired measurements among patients identified as Black and 99 of 2,778 among patients identified as White.”
**Evidence guardrail:** The paper used self-identified race, not direct measurement of skin pigmentation. Race is an imperfect social proxy and must not be presented as a biological correction factor. These percentages are paired measurements in one retrospective cohort, not universal device failure rates.

#### 11. Widen the frame — 8:35–9:45

**On slide:** The finger/device shifts left. Six concentric orbits enter in a rapid automatic cascade:

> LIGHT + SENSOR
> ALGORITHM
> VALIDATION SAMPLE
> LABEL
> PROCUREMENT
> CLINICAL THRESHOLD

**Speaker beat:** “The number is not produced by a clip alone. It is produced by physics, software, who was represented during validation, what performance was labelled, what a hospital bought, and how a clinical pathway uses a threshold.”
**Caution:** Other conditions also affect accuracy, including circulation, skin thickness and temperature, tobacco use, and nail polish. Keep the claim about disparity specific.

#### 12. Audience interaction 1 — Curate the intervention — 9:45–11:50

**On slide:** Four museum cards, each with one action:

> A · REMIND THE CLINICIAN
> B · ADD A WARNING LABEL
> C · REQUIRE REPRESENTATIVE PERFORMANCE TESTING
> D · CHANGE PROCUREMENT + ESCALATION CRITERIA

Prompt:

> You may fund **two**. Which two—and what new failure might remain?

**Facilitation:** Pairs for 55 seconds. Ask one clinical pair and one non-clinical pair for a 20-second answer each; then add one synthesis sentence.
**Speaker synthesis:** “Training and labels may support interpretation. Testing changes what may enter the system. Procurement and escalation change what the organization accepts and how uncertainty is handled. No single card is the whole system.”
**Purpose:** Do not let the exercise become “pick the correct answer.” Reward boundary-widening and remaining-risk thinking.

#### 13. The redesign includes who gets measured — 11:50–13:25

**On slide:** A spectrum of measured skin pigmentation, not racial categories. Over it:

> DESIGN THE EVIDENCE.

Small, progressive labels:

> more participants · pigmentation measured subjectively + objectively · comparable performance labelled

**Speaker beat:** “In January 2025 the FDA issued draft—not final—guidance proposing larger studies, evaluation across skin pigmentation, both the Monk Skin Tone Scale and objective individual typology angle, and prominent labelling when comparable performance is demonstrated.”
**Evidence guardrail:** As verified 12 July 2026, the FDA page still labels this **draft**, “not for implementation,” with non-binding recommendations. Do not narrate the proposals as current mandatory requirements.

### Exhibit 03 — Designed recovery: automated dispensing (13:25–21:25)

#### 14. The cabinet — 13:25–14:05

**On slide:** Full-height ADC drawer front in the dark, rendered at body scale. Label:

> EXHIBIT 03
> **THE CABINET THAT WAS WRONG**

**Speaker beat:** Three seconds of silence. “This time the machine is confident. The humans hesitate.”
**Caution:** The hospital network and vendor are not named in the source. Keep them anonymous.

#### 15. The impossible drawer — 14:05–15:10

**On slide:** Two stacked discordances. The second illuminates automatically after 700 ms:

> Selected: **misoprostol tablets**
> Displayed: **30 mL unit-dose cups**

> Selected: **morphine 10 mg tablets**
> Drawer: **morphine 2 mg ampoules**

**Speaker beat:** “Nursing staff reported both discrepancies within minutes of the upload.” Pause. “Notice what worked before we discuss what failed.”
**Caution:** Do not say a wrong drug was administered. These were detection reports.

#### 16. Widen the frame — 15:10–16:20

**On slide:** A row of medication fields shifts by one position like a typographic frameshift. The drawer stays on the right:

> NAME | STRENGTH | FORMAT | QUANTITY | LOCATION

**Speaker beat:** “The vendor protocol required one complete database import. A partial import followed by a second import misaligned the data. Corrupted information then reached the live network. The process could not be stopped immediately.”
**Caution:** The source identifies a deviation from protocol and live deployment as key factors; do not collapse this into blame of the operator who performed the import.

#### 17. The blast radius — 16:20–17:20

**On slide:** Three statistics occupy three full-height panels. A moving light gives each emphasis in sequence without another click:

> **300+** medication products
> **150+** medication transactions
> **6 hours** of Code Grey

**Speaker beat:** Let each number replace the last. “This was not a drawer problem. It was a networked work-system event.”
**Evidence:** More than 300 products and 150 transactions were affected; the Code Grey took approximately six hours to resolve.

#### 18. The counter-reveal — 17:20–18:30

**On slide:** Hard cut from the scale statistics to:

> **0 reported patient harm**

After an 800 ms automatic delay, add in cyan:

> PEOPLE CAUGHT THE SYSTEM.

**Speaker beat:** “No patient harm was reported. Pharmacy and nursing staff worked together, and the organization activated its emergency plan and operations centre.”
**Caution:** Say **no reported patient harm as a result of the incident**, not “no harm occurred” and not “the system was safe.” The zero belongs beside the scale of exposure and the response, not as reassurance.

#### 19. Audience interaction 2 — Curate the defences — 18:30–20:35

**On slide:** Three empty plinths:

> PREVENT · DETECT · RECOVER

Prompt:

> Place one defence on your assigned plinth.

**Facilitation for nine groups of about five:** Groups 1–3 take **prevent**, 4–6 **detect**, 7–9 **recover**. Give 60 seconds. Ask exactly one group from each category for a 15-second label. Type or handwrite only the noun phrase on the slide.
**Seed answers if the room stalls:** test-environment validation and approval before production; automated checks for illogical units/data; staff escalation; accessible downtime procedures; pharmacy deployment to care areas; emergency operations and communication.
**Speaker synthesis:** “A resilient system does not depend on perfect prevention. It creates multiple chances to notice, stop, adapt, and recover.”
**Guardrail:** Celebrate frontline expertise without making people the permanent compensating control for unsafe technology.

#### 20. The full exhibit label — 20:35–21:25

**On slide:** The actual causal chain, one line only:

> PROTOCOL DEVIATION → DATA MISALIGNMENT → LIVE NETWORK → HUMAN DETECTION → ORGANIZED RECOVERY

**Speaker beat:** “The same story contains both failure and success. A system view must explain both.”
**Purpose:** This is the pivot from error prevention alone to resilience engineering.

---

### Exit gallery — one transferable idea (21:25–25:30)

#### 21. Three objects, three designed realities — 21:25–23:05

**On slide:** The three objects, equal scale in three pools of light:

> DOLORAL — **MEANING**
> PULSE OXIMETER — **MEASUREMENT**
> ADC — **RECOVERY**

After a brief automatic pause, one common caption:

> The action occurred inside a designed world.

**Speaker beat:** “The label shaped what a number meant. The device and its evidence shaped what the body appeared to be doing. The network and emergency plan shaped whether a database failure reached a patient.”
**Check for transfer:** “In each case, was the person outside the system?” Let the room answer: no. “Exactly. The user is part of the system.”

#### 22. Exit question / handoff — 23:05–25:30

**On slide:** The first statement is already present on entry:

> Humans are fallible.
> We will err.

**Speaker beat:** Preserve the original 2025 line and its pause.

After the speaker's first pause, an automatic reveal completes the consequence:

> **Therefore, systems must anticipate, detect, and help us recover.**

At the end of the same timed sequence, `USER ERROR` appears. The **R** exits. The final field becomes:

> **USE ERROR**
> What made this action likely?

**Speaker close:** “The museum is not a collection of foolish people. It is a collection of reasonable actions in designed worlds. For the rest of this course, whenever someone asks, ‘Who failed?’, I want you to widen the frame.”
**Handoff to the next lecture movement:** “Now we need a way to see what enters that frame.” Hard cut to the Human-Tech Ladder or SEIPS—not a new case.

## Evidence and wording guardrails

- **Doloral:** The source describes several reported 11-fold dosing errors, including one reported incident with severe harm. The numeral's proximity was a key contributing factor. Do not infer a population incidence, a single causal mechanism, or a date for each underlying event.
- **Pulse oximetry:** The 11.7% and 3.6% figures are from paired measurements in the University of Michigan cohort when SpO₂ was 92–96% and SaO₂ was below 88%. The study compared self-identified Black and White patients; it did not directly measure skin pigmentation. Use the newer FDA language about performance across a range of skin pigmentations, but label its 2025 guidance as **draft**.
- **ADC:** The 2025 bulletin reports more than 300 products, more than 150 transactions, an approximately six-hour Code Grey, and no reported patient harm from the incident. It does not name the hospital/vendor or say that an incorrect medication reached a patient. The educational payoff is resilient detection and recovery as well as database-deployment failure.
- Across all three, say **“systems shape whether errors are likely, detectable, recoverable, and harmful.”** Avoid “systems cause every error” and “people are never accountable.”

## Build brief for the prototype deck

- Deliver exactly 22 click states at 1920 × 1080. Every state must be legible as a still; reveals should deepen one scene rather than introduce visual clutter.
- Create three master compositions: **label close-up**, **finger/device close-up**, **drawer/body-scale close-up**. Derive all evidence and system reveals from those masters so each object visibly persists while the frame widens.
- Put presenter prompts and caveats in notes. Slides hold at most one sentence, one number pair, or five system nouns.
- Carry a tiny gallery marker—`01/03`, `02/03`, `03/03`—in the upper right. Remove it for the final two slides so the thesis feels larger than the exhibits.
- Test from the back of a 45-seat room. If a citation, unit, or medication form cannot be read at a glance, enlarge it or move it to notes.
- Build a one-page instructor evidence card containing the exact denominators, source language, draft-guidance status, and prohibited overclaims above.
