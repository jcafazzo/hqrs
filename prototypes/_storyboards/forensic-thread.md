# Prototype 1 — The Forensic Thread

## The taste

This is a genuinely independent **28:30 opening**, not the existing opening with new skins. One contemporary event is viewed first as an apparent medication error and then reopened, layer by layer, until the room can no longer sustain a person-first explanation. The story never leaves the case; corridor care is already one of its conditions, and fatigue is introduced only as a later stress-test of the proposed redesign.

The transformation is simple:

> **Whose error was this?** → **What made this action and outcome possible?**

The protected phrase is `USE ERROR`, not `USER ERROR`. The analytical habit is: **widen the frame before assigning judgment**.

### Performance parameters

- **Audience:** 44–45 graduate clinicians and life-science professionals, seated as nine groups of approximately five.
- **Nominal runtime:** 28:30; performance range 26–32 minutes depending on discussion.
- **Format:** 27 projected click-states: 22 narrative beats, five dedicated back-row observation states, and one 3:45 group investigation.
- **Stagecraft:** presenter-led; almost no text that could function as a handout; silence, hard cuts, and causal reveals carry the argument.
- **Case identity:** use “the patient” throughout, as HSSIB does. Do not invent a name, face, or composite biography.
- **Ethical stance:** the event is not entertainment and the patient's death is not used as a causal punchline.

## Visual and motion language

- 16:9, 1920 × 1080. Queen’s Blue `#002452` is the stage, Gold `#FABD0F` the signal, Red `#B90E31` consequence, White evidence space, and `#212121` the on-screen black.
- Use a single monospaced treatment only for clock times and the medication schedule. Everything else is Open Sans or the closest system sans equivalent.
- The recurring object is an **18-position medication timeline**, not a stock photograph. It begins as a pristine schedule and becomes surrounded by system conditions.
- Avoid a literal detective-board aesthetic—no red string, fingerprints, magnifying glasses, or crime imagery. “Forensic” means disciplined reconstruction, not criminality.
- Hard cuts for moral claims; 350–450 ms spatial moves when the system boundary expands; 180–250 ms dissolves for contextual imagery. One causal layer per click.
- Source labels sit at 24–28 px in the lower-left. They remain readable but never compete with the dramatic content.

## 22 narrative beats + 5 full-screen observation states

The numbered beats below remain the performance spine. Five `OBSERVE` states now follow the executive-summary excerpt, SEIPS figure, CARe model, fatigue map, and corridor mitigation photograph so students at the back can inspect the original source at maximum useful size.

### Act I — The attribution trap (00:00–05:30)

#### 1. `08:00` — 00:00–00:20

- **On slide:** black field; `08:00` in warm white, centred, 160–190 px. Nothing else.
- **Speaker beat:** Walk to centre. Let the room settle. “For most medications, eight o’clock is a time.” Pause.
- **Performance:** no build, no logo, no course title. The time itself is the cold open.
- **Evidence/caution:** none yet; this is framing, not a claim.

#### 2. Time is part of the dose — 00:20–00:50

- **On slide:** `For this medication,` then a hard-cut replacement: **`08:00 is part of the dose.`**
- **Speaker beat:** “A medicine can be correct in substance, correct in amount—and still wrong in time.”
- **Evidence/caution:** Parkinson’s medication is treated as time critical in the HSSIB report. Do not imply that every medication has the same timing sensitivity. [P]

#### 3. Eighteen — 00:50–01:20

- **On slide:** an 18-position schedule appears as two clean rows of nine cyan capsules/dots. One word beneath: **`scheduled`**.
- **Speaker beat:** “During one emergency-department stay, this patient was due eighteen doses of co-beneldopa.”
- **Evidence/caution:** HSSIB counted capsules as doses; for example, two strengths due at one administration time count as two doses. Avoid calling this “18 medication rounds.” [P]

#### 4. What happened — 01:20–02:00

- **On slide/build:** seven positions fade to grey and receive a small `MISSED`; three turn amber and receive `LATE`; eight remain cyan. Final line: **`7 missed · 3 late · 8 on time`**.
- **Speaker beat:** State the numbers once. Then silence for three seconds.
- **Evidence/caution:** The report says seven were not given and three were late, meaning only 8 of 18 were provided on time. Do not simplify this to “ten omitted.” [P]

#### 5. Whose error? — 02:00–03:20

- **On slide:** **`Whose error was this?`** Four small choices appear: `patient` · `nurse` · `doctor` · `pharmacy/system`.
- **Speaker beat:** “Do not deliberate. Point to the explanation your mind reached for first.” Ask for hands or one-to-four fingers; scan the room without tallying winners.
- **Interaction:** a 20-second individual attribution, then 30 seconds with the person beside them: “What fact did you assume that I did not give you?” Take two five-second answers.
- **Facilitation guardrail:** never ask a student to defend a blame judgment. The point is to notice the speed of attribution, not to catch the room being immoral.

#### 6. Fallibility is the premise — 03:20–04:00

- **On slide:** **`Humans are fallible.`** Click: **`We will err.`**
- **Speaker beat:** “That includes the patient. The nurse. The physician. The pharmacist. It includes us.” Then add verbally: “Fallibility is a design requirement, not a verdict.”
- **2025 continuity:** keep the existing aphorism and its pause. Its function changes: it is now the premise that opens an investigation rather than the conclusion of one.
- **Caution:** do not let “everyone makes mistakes” become fatalism or absolution. Accountability and system learning are different questions.

#### 7. Remove one letter — 04:00–04:50

- **On slide/build:** `USER ERROR` fills the screen. The coral `R` separates and slides out. The remaining letters close: **`USE ERROR`**. Small line: `The unit of analysis just changed.`
- **Speaker beat:** “That missing R moves our attention from a defective person to an interaction in circumstances.” Pause. “Not who is bad. What made this use—and this outcome—possible?”
- **Evidence/caution:** FDA currently defines use error in the medical-device context as an outcome of use different from what was intended, potentially related to design or a situation promoting incorrect use. This course applies the broader systems habit to clinical work; do not claim the FDA definition formally covers every medication-process failure. [FDA]

### Act II — Widen the frame (04:50–13:30)

#### 8. Fifty-two hours — 04:50–05:30

- **On slide:** the medication timeline shrinks to the upper-left. Centre screen: **`52 hours in the ED`**.
- **Speaker beat:** “Now we widen the frame. This was not one dose at one moment. It was a patient journey through fifty-two hours of emergency care.”
- **Evidence/caution:** verified case fact. [P]

#### 9. Forty-four in a corridor — 05:30–06:20

- **On slide/build:** `52` remains faint. **`44`** expands to hero scale. Beneath: **`hours cared for in a corridor`**.
- **Speaker beat:** “For forty-four of those hours, the care environment was a corridor.” Let the ratio register. “The environment is no longer scenery. It is part of the medication system.”
- **Evidence/caution:** HSSIB says corridor care created additional challenges and **may have** limited opportunities to store medication brought from home. Preserve “may”; the report does not isolate corridor placement as the cause of the missed doses. [P]

#### 10. The environment was not designed for the work — 06:20–07:20

- **On slide:** documentary corridor image or restrained generated scene, darkened. Three lines enter one at a time: **`not designed`** · **`not staffed`** · **`not equipped`** `for this care`.
- **Speaker beat:** “A 2026 HSSIB investigation describes temporary care environments exactly this way. There is a genuine tension: we must not normalize corridor care. But when care is already occurring there, refusing mitigations can also leave patients exposed.”
- **Evidence/caution:** The 2026 report is a multi-hospital investigation separate from the 2024 patient case. Use it to generalize the environmental design problem, not to add unobserved facts to this patient. It reports that fear of normalizing corridor care can inhibit adaptations such as electrical points or emergency call bells. [T]

#### 11. The task was a chain — 07:20–08:20

- **On slide/build:** a five-node line beneath the 18-dose schedule: `IDENTIFY` → `VERIFY` → `PRESCRIBE` → `OBTAIN` → `ADMINISTER`. The chain does not “break”; instead, handoff gaps open between nodes.
- **Speaker beat:** “There was no single act called ‘give the medication.’ There was a distributed chain, crossing people, locations, records and time.”
- **Evidence/caution:** this is a teaching synthesis of the workflow documented by HSSIB, not a validated process map from the report. Label it `reconstructed from HSSIB findings`. [P]

#### 12. The tools changed under the work — 08:20–09:20

- **On slide/build:** two overlapping artifacts appear: `ePMA` and `PAPER`. A small outage symbol sits between them. Then: `NO TIME-CRITICAL ALERT`.
- **Speaker beat:** “The electronic prescribing system was unavailable. Staff needed paper and electronic charts. When it returned, the system still had no function to alert staff that this patient’s medication was time critical.”
- **Evidence/caution:** the outage and absence of a time-critical alert are case findings. HSSIB says the dual charts **may have** caused additional confusion; do not state that the technology caused the missed doses. [P]

#### 13. Ownership was undefined — 09:20–10:20

- **On slide:** the task chain remains. Above it, three role labels—`ED` · `SPECIALTY TEAM` · `PHARMACY`—hover without a connector. Hero line: **`Who owned the clock?`**
- **Speaker beat:** “HSSIB found no defined roles or responsibilities in the ED for identifying, prescribing and ensuring administration of time-critical medication. There was no dedicated pharmacy support.”
- **Evidence/caution:** state this as a property of the investigated work system, not as universal ED practice. [P]

#### 14. The “correct” record was wrong — 10:20–11:30

- **On slide/build:** `SON` and `GP SUMMARY` offer two conflicting dose patterns. A check mark lands on `GP SUMMARY`; on the next click the check becomes **`INCORRECT`** in coral. Around the edge: `clinic-letter backlog` · `specialty service closed` · `GP closed`.
- **Speaker beat:** “The family and record conflicted. Staff chose the apparently authoritative source. It was wrong. The neurology letter was not yet available, and specialist and primary-care services could not be reached outside working hours.”
- **Evidence/caution:** avoid narrating the family as correct in every respect or the clinician as credulous. The report says the sources conflicted and the GP summary record contained incorrect information. [P]

#### 15. SEIPS: the work system appears — 11:30–12:40

- **On slide/build:** the complete configuration locks into a calm ring around the 18-dose timeline:
  - `PEOPLE` patient, family, nurses, doctors, pharmacy
  - `TASKS` identify, verify, prescribe, obtain, administer
  - `TOOLS` ePMA, paper chart, EPR, home medication
  - `ORGANIZATION` role clarity, pharmacy support, service hours
  - `ENVIRONMENT` corridor, medication room, computer access
  - `EXTERNAL` cross-setting records and policies
  Centre: **`performance is conditional`**.
- **Speaker beat:** Name the categories quickly; do not lecture the diagram. “None of these layers excuses an action. Together they explain the conditions under which action was possible.”
- **Evidence/caution:** SEIPS 2.0 is the analytical lens. The exact labels here are adapted for stage legibility, while the complete model includes interacting work-system, process and outcome elements. [SEIPS]

#### 16. Not one broken link — 12:40–13:30

- **On slide:** the ring remains; the task chain disappears. **`No single broken link.`** Click: **`A configuration that could not reliably keep time.`**
- **Speaker beat:** “The seductive question was: who missed the dose? The useful question is: why did this configuration require heroic memory and coordination to keep time?”
- **Caution:** “configuration” is not proof of a single root cause. Avoid root-cause language; the report presents interacting factors and learning prompts.

### Investigation — redesign the conditions (13:30–18:45)

#### 17. Nine groups, three design targets — 13:30–17:15

- **On slide:** three large columns:
  1. **`MAKE IT VISIBLE`** — groups 1, 4, 7
  2. **`MAKE IT OWNED`** — groups 2, 5, 8
  3. **`MAKE IT RECOVERABLE`** — groups 3, 6, 9

  Bottom constraint: **`It must still work at 03:00, in a corridor, during downtime.`**
- **Speaker instructions:**
  1. Number tables 1–9 in advance or point around the room.
  2. Give **90 seconds** to propose one change. Ban “be careful,” “educate,” and “remind staff” as the complete answer.
  3. Give **45 seconds** to stress-test it: “What new failure could your design create?”
  4. Invite one group from each target to report for **20 seconds**; ask the two parallel groups to add only something genuinely different.
- **Purpose:** nine groups work simultaneously on one case rather than generating nine unrelated anecdotes. Repeating each target three times exposes variation without a long report-back.
- **Facilitation move:** if a group offers an alert, ask: “Who receives it, when, and what closes the loop?” If a group offers policy, ask: “What makes the safe action easier in the corridor at 03:00?”

#### 18. Convert ideas into system properties — 17:15–18:45

- **On slide/build:** capture or verbally place responses under three verbs:
  - **`DETECT`** — time-critical status and schedule become salient at arrival
  - **`OWN`** — responsibility and escalation are explicit
  - **`RECOVER`** — downtime, after-hours, corridor and appropriate self-administration have a safe path
- **Speaker beat:** “These are not three products. They are three properties we want the work system to have.” Connect good student proposals to HSSIB's learning prompts about identification, responsibility, pharmacy support, ePMA alerts, downtime, information access and supported self-administration.
- **Evidence/caution:** HSSIB's prompts are improvement prompts, not proof that any one proposed design reduces harm. Present the slide as a design brief awaiting local co-design and evaluation. [P]

### Act III — design for the worst ordinary day (18:45–25:30)

#### 19. Fatigue: evidence discipline and a stress-test — 18:45–20:15

- **On slide:** **`FATIGUE`**. Beneath in two lines: `a real system hazard` / **`not established in this case`**. Click: **`Would your design still work?`**
- **Speaker beat:** “We must not retrofit fatigue into an event because it makes the story persuasive. This investigation did not establish it. A separate 2025 HSSIB investigation found fatigue under-recognized and poorly captured, and recommended a systems approach to managing it. So fatigue becomes a stress-test: would your intervention survive a tired person, or does it demand more vigilance?”
- **Evidence/caution:** keep the distinction explicit. Do not merge the 2025 fatigue investigation with the 2024 Parkinson’s case. The fatigue report says the size and scale of harm remain poorly understood; it used SEIPS to examine personal and organizational factors. [F]

#### 20. Which intervention survives? — 20:15–22:00

- **On slide/build:** three responses appear vertically:
  1. `REMEMBER TO BE CAREFUL` — fades
  2. `ADD AN ALERT` — remains but shows `? ownership · ? escalation · ? overload`
  3. **`MAKE TIME CRITICAL VISIBLE + OWNED + RECOVERABLE`** — dark-teal field

  Final question: **`Which survives crowding, downtime and fatigue?`**
- **Speaker beat:** “Education and alerts can be useful. The test is whether they leave success dependent on attention at the worst moment.” This is a teaser for the later Hierarchy of Intervention Effectiveness, not the full HIE lecture.
- **Caution:** do not imply that every alert is weak or that the third line is one ready-to-install intervention. Intervention strength is contextual and must be tested for new failure modes.

#### 21. A safer configuration — 22:00–23:45

- **On slide/build:** a closed-loop pathway replaces the fragmented chain:

  `ARRIVAL` → `TIME-CRITICAL STATUS` → `NAMED OWNER` → `DOSE DUE`

  From `DOSE DUE`, two equal paths: `ADMINISTER + RECORD` or `ESCALATE + RECOVER`. A quiet lower rail remains active throughout: `DOWNTIME · CORRIDOR · AFTER HOURS · SUPPORTED SELF-ADMINISTRATION`.

  Hero footer: **`anticipate · detect · recover`**.
- **Speaker beat:** “This is not the solution. It is the specification: make time visible, close ownership, and create recovery paths before the ordinary system degrades.” Invite the class to notice that the person remains inside the system rather than disappearing from it.
- **Evidence/caution:** explicitly label `concept design, not validated intervention`. It synthesizes HSSIB learning prompts and student proposals. Local workflow study, co-design, usability testing, balancing measures and outcome evaluation would be required. [P]

#### 22. The new habit — 23:45–25:30

- **On slide/build:** black field. `USER ERROR` appears for one beat; the `R` drops out. **`USE ERROR`** remains. Click to final line: **`People will err. Systems decide what happens next.`**
- **Speaker beat:** “For the rest of this course, before we ask who failed, we will ask what conditions shaped the action—what made error likely, whether it could be detected, whether recovery was possible, and whether it reached the patient.” Hold the final screen for five seconds. Transition: “Now let’s learn how to see those conditions.”
- **Caution:** say verbally, if needed: “System learning does not erase professional accountability. It makes accountability better informed.”

## Runtime controls

- **21–23 minute cut:** reduce state 5 to a silent show of hands; give groups 60 seconds plus two reports; combine states 9 and 10; deliver state 19 as a 30-second evidence guardrail.
- **28–29 minute stretch:** take one additional group response per design target and ask a nurse, physician and allied-health participant to name one different work-system constraint. Do not add another case.
- **If discussion becomes blame-focused:** return to state 15 and ask, “What information and resources were available to that person at that moment?” Do not debate character or intent.
- **If discussion becomes solutioneering:** return to state 18 and ask which property—detect, own or recover—the idea creates, and what new failure it might introduce.

## Claims that must remain precise

1. Say **“only 8 of 18 doses were provided on time”**, not “the hospital gave only eight doses.” Three doses were self-administered and the report reconstructs the record with stated uncertainty.
2. Say the corridor **may have limited** medication storage and created additional challenges; do not claim it caused the missed doses.
3. Say the dual paper/electronic chart **may have caused additional confusion**; do not claim the outage caused the event.
4. Do not say missed medication caused the patient’s death. HSSIB reports that the patient died four weeks later and lists bronchopneumonia, Parkinson’s and frailty on the death certificate. The opening is about work-system conditions, not causal adjudication of death.
5. Do not say staff fatigue contributed to this event. Fatigue is deliberately used as a separate, evidence-labelled robustness test.
6. The proposed closed-loop workflow is a **concept specification**, not a proven intervention.
7. Systems analysis and individual accountability are not opposites. The lecture's purpose is learning and safer design; culpability requires a separate, fair process with fuller evidence.

- **[T] HSSIB (2026), _Patient care in temporary care environments_.** The report supports the description of corridors and other temporary spaces as not originally designed, staffed or equipped for care, the need to manage system-wide risk, and the tension between non-normalization and practical safety mitigations.
  <https://www.hssib.org.uk/patient-safety-investigations/patient-care-in-temporary-care-environments/investigation-report/>

- **[F] HSSIB (2025), _The impact of staff fatigue on patient safety_.** The report supports treating fatigue as an under-recognized systems hazard, acknowledges poor measurement of its scale, uses SEIPS, and recommends systems-oriented fatigue-risk management.
  <https://www.hssib.org.uk/patient-safety-investigations/the-impact-of-staff-fatigue-on-patient-safety/investigation-report/>

## Build brief for the visual prototype

The HTML/slide render should make states 1–7, 9–15, 17, 19–22 directly navigable. The medication timeline should be a reusable SVG component so the same 18 positions persist spatially while the frame widens. The SEIPS ring in state 15 should be assembled from the exact visual fragments introduced in states 9–14; nothing new should appear when the system “locks.” The final `USER ERROR` → `USE ERROR` animation should be interruptible and should still read correctly with reduced motion, where the `R` simply cuts away. Build presenter notes separately; do not place the evidence guardrails on the audience-facing slide beyond a short source label and the explicit `not established in this case` line on fatigue.
